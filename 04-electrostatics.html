    The Coulomb Problem

    Electrostatic interactions are long-range:

    \[V(r) = k\frac{q_1 q_2}{r}\]

    Unlike LJ (which decays as r⁻⁶), Coulomb falls off slowly. In a periodic system, the sum of all pairwise Coulomb interactions does not converge quickly — you cannot simply cut it off at a reasonable distance without introducing large errors.

    Direct summation Cost Problem
    All pairs (N²/2) O(N²) Prohibitive for large N
    Cutoff at 10 Å O(N) ~10–50% error for ionic systems

    The challenge: we need the long-range part of Coulomb for accuracy, but we cannot sum it directly because there are too many pairs.

    Why Coulomb is harder than LJ: - For unit charges at 3 Å: V ≈ 110 kcal/mol (real units) - For LJ minimum at same distance: V ≈ -1 kcal/mol - Coulomb is 100× larger and decays slower

    Ewald Summation — The Conceptual Basis

    The Split

    Ewald summation splits the Coulomb sum into three parts:

    \[V_{Coul} = V_{real} + V_{recip} + V_{self}\]

    Term Description How It’s Computed
    Real space Short-range Coulomb (direct sum) Cutoff-based; fast for small r
    Reciprocal space Long-range (Fourier transform) FFT-based; fast for large N
    Self term Corrects for double-counting Analytic formula

    The Physical Idea

    1. Smear each charge into a Gaussian cloud (width determined by α)
    2. Real space: interaction of Gaussians with original charges (short-range, convergent)
    3. Reciprocal space: interaction of Gaussians with each other in Fourier space (long-range, also convergent)
    4. Self term: subtract the interaction of each charge with its own Gaussian

    Why the Split Works

    The problem with direct Coulomb sum is that \(1/r\) is slowly decaying. By smearing charges into Gaussians:

    • The short-range interaction (original charge × Gaussian of neighbor) converges quickly
    • The long-range interaction (Gaussian × Gaussian) becomes smooth and amenable to Fourier methods

    The Ewald parameter α controls the split: - Large α: Gaussian is narrow → real-space converges fast → reciprocal space slow - Small α: Gaussian is wide → real-space converges slow → reciprocal space fast

    For typical systems, α ≈ 0.2–0.5 Å⁻¹ (real units). LAMMPS chooses a reasonable default based on the system size and cutoff.

    Ewald in LAMMPS

    kspace_style ewald 1e-5

    1e-5 = desired RMS force accuracy (relative to force magnitude). Tighter tolerance = more accurate = slower.

    How to interpret tolerance: If the RMS force on atoms is ~100 kcal/mol/Å, a tolerance of 1e-5 means the kspace force error is ~0.001 kcal/mol/Å (negligible).

    PPPM — Particle-Particle Particle-Mesh

    Why PPPM?

    Ewald scales as O(N³/²). For very large systems, this is still prohibitive.

    PPPM (Particle-Particle Particle-Mesh, also called PME — Particle Mesh Ewald) maps charges to a regular mesh and uses FFT for the reciprocal space sum:

    \[O(N \log N) \text{ scaling}\]

    How PPPM Works

    1. Distribute charges to mesh: each charge assigned to nearby mesh points (Gaussian or cloud-in-cell scheme)
    2. FFT to reciprocal space: ρ(r) → ρ(k) (charge density in k-space)
    3. Multiply by Green’s function: apply the Coulomb kernel \(4π/k²\) in k-space
    4. FFT back to real space: get the long-range potential on the mesh
    5. Interpolate forces to particles: mesh → atom forces via shape function

    The key insight: convolution with Green’s function becomes multiplication in Fourier space. FFT does this efficiently.

    PPPM in LAMMPS

    kspace_style pppm 1e-5

    For ELECTRODE systems, use the electrode-aware variant:

    kspace_style pppm/electrode 1e-5

    PPPM Accuracy vs. Speed

    Tolerance Force RMS Error Relative Error Speed
    1e-3 ~1% Large Fastest
    1e-4 ~0.1% Noticeable Fast
    1e-5 ~0.01% Usually fine Medium
    1e-6 ~0.001% Precise Slow

    For production: 1e-5 is standard. For precise energy calculations (e.g., comparing binding energies): 1e-6.

    Cutoff-Based Methods (When to Use Them)

    Direct Coulomb with Cutoff

    pair_style lj/cut/coul/cut 10.0   # real-space only; no kspace
kspace_style none                   # explicitly no long-range

    DO NOT use for charged systems or electrolytes. The long-range part is significant — cutting it off introduces errors of 10–50%.

    When Cutoff-Based Is Acceptable

    • Neutral, sparse systems (gases at low pressure)
    • Very short simulations where speed matters more than accuracy
    • Testing/debugging before production
    • Systems with all charges screened by high dielectric (but even then, be careful)

    The Error from Truncating Coulomb

    For 1M NaCl solution: - Bulk screening length ≈ 10 Å - But at electrode surfaces, unscreened charges persist - Cutting off at 10 Å means you miss the long-range surface interactions entirely - This changes capacitance by 20–50%

    Conclusion: for electrode-electrolyte simulations, always use PPPM.

    Boundary Conditions and Electrostatics

    Periodic Boundary Conditions in 3D

    With boundary p p p, the system is a periodic tile repeating infinitely. Ewald/PPPM works straightforwardly.

    Slab Geometry (Non-Periodic Z)

    For electrode-electrolyte simulations:

    boundary p p f    # non-periodic z

    The z-direction has a vacuum gap. This creates two electrode surfaces exposed to vacuum (or vacuum region above electrolyte).

    Problem with slab geometry: electrostatics in 2D is different from 3D. The naive Ewald/PPPM sum would include artificial dipole-dipole interactions between periodic images in z.

    Why this matters: Without correction, the computed electrostatic energy depends on how many times you replicate the cell in z — clearly wrong for a single interface.

    Solution: 2D Ewald / slab correction

    kspace_modify slab 3

    This applies a correction for the slab geometry: - Assumes the system is infinitely periodic in x, y - Assumes finite (non-periodic) in z - Adds a dipole correction term that accounts for the missing images

    The number 3 refers to the dimensionality being treated as 2D (slab). LAMMPS automatically handles the correction.

    WarningForgot kspace_modify slab?

    If you use boundary p p f without kspace_modify slab, you will get: - Artificially high pressure in z-direction - Incorrect forces on atoms near the surface - Wrong double-layer structure and capacitance

    Always add kspace_modify slab 3 (or equivalent) for non-periodic z.

    The Dipole Correction

    The slab correction adds a term that accounts for the net dipole moment of the slab interacting with its periodic images. It has the form:

    \[V_{dipole} = \frac{2\pi}{V} \left|\sum_i q_i z_i\right|^2\]

    Where the sum is over all charges in the cell. This term is added to the reciprocal-space energy.

    Physical interpretation: a polar slab has a dipole moment. In 3D periodic boundaries, that dipole would interact with infinitely many images. In slab geometry, only the x,y periodicity remains, and the correction accounts for the missing z-interactions.

    Periodic Z with Finite Field

    boundary p p p    # fully periodic
# In fix electrode/conp:
fix fxconp bot electrode/conp -1.0 1.805 couple top 1.0 ffield on

    With ffield on, the system is treated as an infinite stack of identical cells: - No vacuum region needed - Smaller z-dimension possible (saves atoms) - Requires symm on (total charge neutrality) - Uses internal E-field to apply potential difference: \(E = V/d\)

    Advantages: - Better scaling for large systems (fully periodic) - No slab correction needed - Smaller cell means fewer atoms

    Disadvantages: - Electrodes must be symmetric (infinite stack assumption) - Can’t model single-surface experiments - Requires careful treatment of dipole moment

    Pair Style — Kspace Consistency

    The Rule

    The pair style determines what forces are computed in real space. The kspace style handles the long-range correction.

    If you use coul/long or coul/long as part of a pair style, LAMMPS requires an appropriate kspace style. The long suffix means “long-range handled by kspace”.

    Pair Style Real Space Kspace Style Notes
    lj/cut/coul/long LJ + Coulomb (short-range) pppm or ewald Standard for electrolytes
    lj/cut/coul/cut LJ + Coulomb (truncated) none Large error; only for testing
    coul/long only Coulomb (short-range only) pppm or ewald No LJ
    lj/cut only LJ only none No electrostatics

    Why Consistency Matters

    If you use lj/cut/coul/long but no kspace style:

    ERROR: Kspace style required for coul/long pair style

    LAMMPS will error. You must add kspace.

    If you use lj/cut/coul/cut but kspace_style pppm:

    The truncated Coulomb in real space already includes some long-range contribution. Adding PPPM double-counts or produces wrong results. LAMMPS may warn but will likely compute nonsense.

    Always match pair style to kspace style: coul/long → pppm/ewald; coul/cut → none.

    Electrode-Specific Kspace Styles

    For ELECTRODE simulations, you MUST use electrode-aware kspace styles:

    kspace_style pppm/electrode 1e-5
kspace_style ewald/electrode 1e-5

    These variants: - Provide electrode-electrode interaction matrix to the ELECTRODE fix - Handle the induced-charge electrostatics correctly (electrode charges are not fixed) - Are compatible with electrode/conp, electrode/conq, electrode/thermo

    Standard pppm or ewald will error with ELECTRODE fixes.

    Kspace Modify Options

    kspace_modify slab 3              # 2D Ewald for non-periodic z (default = 3)
kspace_modify wire                 # wire boundary conditions
kspace_modify one-step            # faster but less accurate elastance matrix
kspace_modify force 1e-4          # override tolerance
kspace_modify order 5             # PPPM grid order (higher = more accurate but slower)
    Option Purpose When to Use
    slab 3 2D Ewald correction Non-periodic z (slab geometry)
    wire Wire boundary conditions Cylindrical geometry
    one-step Faster elastance matrix calculation Large electrode systems; less accuracy needed
    force Override tolerance When you want different accuracy than global setting
    order PPPM interpolation order Higher = more accurate but slower (default = 5)

    Output and Diagnostics

    Kspace Energy

    In thermodynamic output:

    Kspace = 0.5 * Σ_k G(k) * |ρ(k)|²

    KspaceEng = reciprocal-space energy contribution.

    Large KspaceEng (relative to PairEng) indicates: - Many charged particles - Weak screening - Strong long-range interactions

    For pure water at 1 g/cm³, KspaceEng is typically ~30% of PairEng. For ionic solutions, it can be 50%+.

    Checking Energy Conservation

    With PPPM, energy is not strictly conserved (unlike NVE with exact Ewald). But drift should be small:

    # NVE with PPPM to check drift
fix conservative all nve
run 10000
# Check TotEng drift over this run

    Drift > 0.1% over 10 ps indicates a problem (timestep, tolerance, overlapping atoms).

    Performance

    Kspace Style Scaling N = 1000 N = 10000 N = 100000
    ewald O(N³/²) ~1s/step ~10s/step ~100s/step
    pppm O(N log N) ~0.5s ~2s ~10s

    For electrode systems with 10,000+ atoms, PPPM is essential.

    Grid Size Effects

    PPPM uses a mesh. Too coarse mesh = inaccurate. LAMMPS chooses mesh size based on tolerance and cutoff.

    kspace_modify mesh 32 32 32    # manually set mesh (not usually needed)

    If you see “PPPM grid too small” warnings, the tolerance may be too tight for the mesh.

    Common Issues

    Problem Cause Solution
    Very large KspaceEng kspace tolerance too loose Use 1e-6 or tighter
    Pressure oscillations in z Slab correction wrong Check kspace_modify slab
    Energy not conserving Missing kspace or wrong pair style Add kspace or check pair style
    Slow performance Using ewald instead of pppm Switch to pppm
    NaN forces Overlapping atoms Minimize before dynamics
    kspace style not supported Using standard pppm with ELECTRODE Use pppm/electrode
    Warnings about PPPM accuracy Grid too coarse for tolerance Use tighter tolerance or larger grid
