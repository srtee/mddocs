What Is a Force Field?

A force field is a set of mathematical functions and parameters that describe interatomic forces. It is an approximation — no force field is “correct”, only more or less appropriate for a given system and question.

The force field determines:

  1. What interactions exist — which pairs have attractive/repulsive forces
  2. How strong they are — the energy scale of binding
  3. What is neglected — polarizability, electronic structure, chemical reactions

Force Field Components

Component What It Describes Typical Functional Form
Non-bonded Van der Waals, electrostatics LJ, Coulomb
Bonded Chemical bonds Harmonic, Morse
Angle Three-body bending Harmonic, cosine
Dihedral Four-body torsion Trigonometric series

For simple electrolytes (salt in water), only non-bonded interactions are needed. For explicit water models, bonds and angles are also required.

Non-Bonded Interactions

Van der Waals (Dispersion/Repulsion)

The Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential is the workhorse of MD:

\[V(r) = 4\epsilon \left[ \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^6 \right]\]

Parameter Physical Meaning Typical Values (real units)
\(\epsilon\) Depth of energy well (binding strength) 0.1–0.2 kcal/mol for atoms
\(\sigma\) Distance where energy = zero 3.0–4.0 Å for light atoms

Term-by-Term Breakdown

Term Physical Origin Behavior
\(r^{-12}\) Pauli exclusion (electron cloud repulsion) Very steep; only matters at short range
\(r^{-6}\) London dispersion (induced dipole attraction) Softer; dominant at intermediate range

The r⁻¹² term is 4096× steeper than r⁻⁶ at r = σ. This is why LJ produces a hard-sphere-like repulsion at short distances while having a soft attractive well.

Why r⁻¹² specifically? It’s computationally convenient (easy to square), not because it represents any specific physical interaction. Other repulsion forms (Born-Mayer, exp) are more physically grounded but less common.

Visual Representation

Key Properties of LJ

Property Value Significance
Energy minimum \(V_{min} = -\epsilon\) at \(r_{min} = 2^{1/6}\sigma \approx 1.12\sigma\) Determines equilibrium spacing
Zero crossing \(r = \sigma\) Convenient reference point
Force zero \(r = 2^{1/6}\sigma\) Where attraction balances repulsion

LJ in LAMMPS

pair_style lj/cut 10.0    # LJ with cutoff at 10 Å
pair_coeff 1 1 0.15 3.5   # epsilon=0.15, sigma=3.5 (real units)

Cutoff considerations: - LJ is truncated at rcut — introduces error, but necessary for speed - Typical rcut = 10–12 Å for water; energy drift < 0.1% if cutoff is appropriate - Never exceed half the minimum box dimension — you would double-count interactions - Some use “shifted” LJ to make potential go smoothly to zero at cutoff: lammps pair_style lj/cut/coul/long 10.0 # no shift pair_style lj/charmm/coul/long 10.0 12.0 # with shift

Coulombic Electrostatics

\[V(r) = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r}\]

Property Value
Constant \(k\) 332.07 Å·kcal/mol·e² (real) or 14.3996 Å·eV·e² (metal)
Decay \(r^{-1}\) — long-range, never truly zero
Energy scale Much larger than LJ for charged systems

Why Coulomb is the hard problem: A pair of unit charges at 3 Å has \(V\) ≈ 110 kcal/mol (real units) — far larger than LJ well depths. And it doesn’t decay to zero at typical cutoffs.

This is why special methods (Ewald, PPPM) are needed — see ?@sec-electrostatics.

In LAMMPS

pair_style lj/cut/coul/long 10.0   # LJ + real-space Coulomb
pair_coeff * * 0.15 3.5            # LJ for all pairs
pair_coeff 1 1 0.0 0.0             # no LJ for specific pairs
kspace_style pppm 1e-5             # long-range solver (REQUIRED)

Key rule: If you use coul/long, you MUST use a kspace style like pppm or ewald. The long designation means “long-range handled by kspace”, not “I don’t need kspace”.

Pair Style Compatibility

The pair style determines what is computed in real space. The kspace style must complement it:

Pair Style Real Space Kspace Style Notes
lj/cut/coul/long LJ + Coulomb (short-range) pppm or ewald Standard for electrolytes
lj/cut/coul/cut LJ + Coulomb (truncated) none Large error; only for testing
coul/long only Coulomb (short-range only) pppm or ewald No LJ
lj/cut only LJ only none No electrostatics
WarningMismatch Causes Error or Crash

If you use lj/cut/coul/long but forget kspace, LAMMPS will error: “Kspace style required for coul/long pair style.”

If you use lj/cut/coul/cut but have kspace, the long-range Coulomb is double-counted or handled incorrectly.

Other Non-Bonded Styles

For specialized systems:

pair_style born/coul/long 10.0    # Born-Mayer repulsion + Coulomb (ionic crystals)
pair_style 12-6-4/long 10.0       # Adds 1/r⁴ term (metal oxides, halides)
pair_style buck/coul/long 10.0   # Buckingham (exp) repulsion + Coulomb

Bonded Interactions

When molecules have explicit chemical bonds (water, organics), bonded terms are needed.

Bonds

Harmonic bond (most common):

\[V(r) = k (r - r_0)^2\]

Parameter Physical Meaning Typical Values (real)
\(k\) Stiffness (force constant) 300–600 kcal/mol/Å²
\(r_0\) Equilibrium bond length 0.96–1.0 Å for O-H

In LAMMPS:

bond_style harmonic
bond_coeff 1 300 1.0    # k=300, r0=1.0 Å

Why harmonic? It’s a good approximation near the minimum. For large deviations, Morse or cubic terms are better.

Angles

Harmonic angle:

\[V(\theta) = k (\theta - \theta_0)^2\]

Parameter Typical Values
\(k\) 30–60 kcal/mol/rad²
\(\theta_0\) ~104.5° for water H-O-H 
angle_style harmonic
angle_coeff 1 40 109.5    # k=40 kcal/mol/rad², theta0=109.5°

Dihedrals

Proper dihedral (torsion around a bond):

\[V(\phi) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} k_n \cos(n\phi - \phi_n)\]

Style Use Case
opls OPLS force field; multiple terms
charmm CHARMM; Fourier series
harmonic Simple harmonic dihedral 
dihedral_style opls
dihedral_coeff 1 0.5 0.0 3 0.0    # k, n, phi0 per term

When Bonded Interactions Matter

System Bonds/Angles Needed?
Simple electrolyte (NaCl in water) No — only non-bonded
SPC/E water Yes — 3 atoms, 2 bonds, 1 angle
TIP4P water Yes — 4 atoms, 2 bonds, 1 angle (rigid)
Organic molecules Yes — for conformational properties
Ionic crystals (NaCl) Typically no — implicit in pair potential
NoteWater Model Checklist

For a new water model, check: 1. How many atoms? → atom_style charge (3 or 4 sites) 2. Are there bonds? → bond_style + bond_coeff if yes 3. Are there angles? → angle_style + angle_coeff if yes 4. Is it rigid? → Use fix rigid or fix shake instead of dynamics on bonds

SPC/E and TIP4P are commonly used rigid models — no bond/angle dynamics needed.

Cross-Terms and Mixing Rules

When two different atom types interact, we need cross-pair parameters.

Lorentz-Berthelot Mixing

Default mixing rule in most force fields:

\[\epsilon_{ij} = \sqrt{\epsilon_{ii} \cdot \epsilon_{jj}}\] \[\sigma_{ij} = \frac{\sigma_{ii} + \sigma_{jj}}{2}\]

Physical meaning: - \(\sigma\) averaging = geometric mean of sizes (reasonable for van der Waals radii) - \(\epsilon\) averaging = arithmetic mean of energy depths

In LAMMPS, this is the default. If you specify only pair_coeff 1 1 and pair_coeff 2 2, the cross-term is computed automatically.

Explicit Cross-Parameters

Sometimes mixing rules give poor results. Override with explicit values:

pair_coeff 1 1 0.01 3.0    # type 1 - type 1
pair_coeff 2 2 0.05 3.5    # type 2 - type 2
pair_coeff 1 2 0.025 3.25  # type 1 - type 2 (explicit override)

When explicit is better: - Electrode-electrolyte interactions (metal-water cross terms often poorly described by mixing) - Systems with very different chemistries - When fitted to quantum calculations or experiment

Force Field Parameters — Where They Come From

Water Models

Water models are parameterized to reproduce: - Density of liquid water at 298 K and 1 atm - Heat of vaporization - Dielectric constant (for polarizable models)

Model Sites Bonds Angles ε (kcal/mol) σ (Å) Notes
SPC 3 2 1 0.155 3.166 Rigid; 3-site pioneer
SPC/E 3 2 1 0.155 3.166 SPC with corrected energy
TIP3P 3 2 1 0.155 3.150 CHARMM version; O-H=0.957Å
TIP4P 4 2 1 0.155 3.154 4-site; M-site for electrostatics
TIP5P 5 2 1 0.155 3.12 5-site; better for ice

SPC/E vs TIP4P: TIP4P gives better density (~1.0 g/cm³ vs ~0.98 for SPC/E) but is slower (extra site). For electrode simulations, SPC/E is common.

Ion Parameters

Ions are parameterized to match: - Solvation free energy - Ion-water RDF from quantum calculations - Hydration structure

From Joung & Cheatham (2008) for SPC/E:

Ion ε (kcal/mol) σ (Å) Notes
Na⁺ 0.0874 3.40 Small; tightly hydrated
K⁺ 0.100 4.47 Larger; more diffuse hydration
Cl⁻ 0.100 4.45 Similar size to K⁺
Mg²⁺ 0.111 3.47 Small; highly charged
Ca²⁺ 0.449 2.95 Divalent; strong binding

Always match ion parameters to your water model. Using NaCl parameters fitted for SPC/E with TIP4P water will give incorrect behavior.

Electrode Parameters

Electrodes in classical MD are typically: - Neutral atoms (no Coulomb contribution — metal screens electrostatics) - Soft repulsion (large ε prevents penetration; not real bonding) - No explicit electronic structure (classical approximation)

Parameter Typical Values (metal units) Physical Effect
ε 0.01–0.1 eV Soft repulsion; prevents electrolyte penetration
σ 2.5–3.5 Å Atomic size; matches electrode lattice constant
q 0 (for standard LJ) No Coulomb; screening is implicit in dielectric

What LJ on electrodes does NOT model: actual metallic bonding, d-band chemistry, charge-transfer (CPM handles this separately).

Force Field → Output Connection

Understanding how force field choices affect observable properties:

Input Change Physical Effect Observable Change
Increase ε (LJ) Stronger binding; lower energy Lower diffusivity; higher density
Increase σ Larger effective size Lower density; slower diffusion
Increase cutoff More accurate (to a point) Lower energy drift; slower
Add bond constraints Remove fast vibrations Can use larger Δt; affects vibrational spectra
Change water model Different H-bonding structure Different density, diffusion, dielectric

Energy Components Breakdown

Potential Energy = PairEng + BondEng + AngleEng + DihedralEng + KspaceEng
                 = (LJ + Coulomb_real) + bonded terms + Coulomb_recip

For a typical electrolyte system:

Component Typical Fraction Notes
PairEng 60–80% Dominates for simple electrolytes
KspaceEng 20–40% Significant for charged systems
BondEng 0–5% Only if flexible molecules
AngleEng 0–2% Only if flexible angles

Large BondEng or AngleEng = atoms too close or bonds overstretched. Check geometry.

Common Force Field Configurations

Simple Electrolyte (NaCl in SPC/E water)

units real
atom_style charge
pair_style lj/cut/coul/long 10.0

# Ion LJ (Joung & Cheatham for SPC/E)
pair_coeff Na Na 0.0874 3.40
pair_coeff Cl Cl 0.100 4.45
pair_coeff Na Cl 0.0935 3.925    # Lorentz-Berthelot mixed

# Water LJ (SPC/E)
pair_coeff OW OW 0.155 3.166    # water oxygen
pair_coeff HW HW 0.0 0.0         # water hydrogen (no LJ)

# Cross terms
pair_coeff Na OW 0.114 3.28
pair_coeff Cl OW 0.124 3.81

kspace_style pppm 1e-5
kspace_modify order 5    # for better accuracy with ions

Electrode-Electrolyte Interface

units metal
atom_style charge
pair_style lj/cut/coul/long 10.0

# Electrode-electrode (soft repulsion, not bonding)
pair_coeff 1 1 0.01 3.0          # Au-Au typical values
pair_coeff 2 2 0.01 3.0          # top electrode same type
pair_coeff 1 2 0.005 3.0         # cross-interaction

# Electrolyte LJ (SPC/E water)
pair_coeff 3 3 0.155 3.166       # water O
pair_coeff 4 4 0.0 0.0          # water H (no LJ)

# Electrode-electrolyte cross terms
# (explicit is often better than Lorentz-Berthelot)
pair_coeff 1 3 0.005 3.1         # electrode1 - water
pair_coeff 2 3 0.005 3.1         # electrode2 - water
pair_coeff 1 4 0.0 0.0           # no LJ with H
pair_coeff 2 4 0.0 0.0

kspace_style pppm/electrode 1e-5
kspace_modify slab 3

Checking Your Force Field

# After setup, use these to verify:
compute pe all pe/atom              # per-atom potential energy
compute ke all ke/atom             # per-atom kinetic energy

# Sum should match thermo PotEng + KinEng
# Run for a few steps and check:
print "Total PE: $(c_pe)"
print "Total KE: $(c_ke)"
