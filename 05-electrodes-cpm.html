    Conductors vs. Insulators in Molecular Dynamics

    Insulator: Fixed Charges

    In a typical molecular simulation (water, proteins, simple electrolytes):

    • Atomic charges are fixed at their initial values
    • The electrostatic potential within the material varies freely depending on charge distribution
    • No charge redistribution occurs during the simulation

    This is appropriate for insulators, where charges cannot move through the material.

    Conductor: Induced Charges

    In a metal (electrode):

    • Charges (electrons) can move freely within the material
    • The material adjusts its charge distribution to equalize the electrostatic potential
    • This happens on timescales much faster than atomic motion (effectively instantaneous)

    If we treat a metal electrode with fixed charges, we’re getting the physics wrong — the electrode cannot maintain arbitrary charge distributions.

    The key difference: In a conductor, the electrostatic potential is the same throughout the material. In an insulator, charges are fixed and potentials can vary.

    The Electrode-Electrolyte Interface

    Structure of the Interface

    When an electrode is placed in electrolyte:

    1. Electric double layer: ions accumulate near the charged surface
    2. Solvation shells: water molecules orient near the surface
    3. Applied potential: changing the electrode voltage changes ion adsorption

    The structure of the double layer determines the capacitance — a key measurable quantity in electrochemistry.

    What Control Do We Have?

    In real electrochemistry, the potential (voltage) of the electrode is the controlled variable, measured and maintained by a potentiostat.

    In a typical MD simulation with fixed atom charges, we can only control the total charge on the electrode — not the potential. This is wrong for electrochemistry.

    Controlled Variable Physical Reality Fixed-Charge MD Constant Potential
    Electrode potential What the potentiostat controls ❌ Cannot enforce ✅ Direct control
    Electrode charge Flows from potential ✅ Can set ❌ Fluctuates

    Why Fixed Charge Fails for Electrodes

    Consider a simulation of charging a supercapacitor:

    1. We start with neutral electrodes
    2. We want to apply 1 V between electrodes
    3. With fixed charges: we must choose some total charge on each electrode — but what charge gives 1 V?
    4. The relationship between charge and potential depends on the electrolyte structure (which we don’t know a priori)

    With constant potential, the charges emerge from the physics — we specify the voltage and the simulation determines the induced charge.

    Example: The same 1 V applied across: - Vacuum → no charge flows (capacitance ~0) - Ionic liquid → large induced charge (high capacitance) - Aqueous salt → moderate charge (intermediate capacitance)

    The capacitance depends on the electrolyte. Fixed charge can’t capture this.

    The Constant Potential Method: Theory

    The Problem Formulation

    We have a system with: - Electrode atoms with unknown charges \(q_i\) (to be determined) - Electrolyte atoms with known charges \(Q_j\) (fixed) - A constraint: each electrode surface must be at potential \(V\)

    The electrostatic energy in matrix form:

    \[E(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{Q}) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{q}^T \mathbf{A} \mathbf{q} + \mathbf{q}^T \mathbf{A} \mathbf{Q} + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{Q}^T \mathbf{A} \mathbf{Q}\]

    Where \(\mathbf{A}\) is the inverse capacitance matrix (from the kspace solver). This comes from solving Poisson’s equation with the system geometry.

    We want to minimize \(E\) subject to the constraint that each electrode group is at target potential \(V_k\).

    Energy Minimization via Lagrange Multipliers

    The mathematical approach uses Lagrange multipliers. We minimize:

    \[\mathcal{L} = E(\mathbf{q}) + \sum_k \lambda_k (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{q})_k - V_k\]

    Where \(\lambda_k\) are Lagrange multipliers enforcing \(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{q}\).

    Taking \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{q} = 0\):

    \[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{q} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{A}^T \boldsymbol{\lambda} = 0\]

    But this simplifies: the constraint is \(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{q}\), so \(\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \mathbf{V}\) and we get:

    \[\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{V} - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q})\]

    The matrix \(\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{C}\) is the capacitance matrix. The induced electrode charges depend on: - The applied potential \(\mathbf{V}\) - The electrolyte configuration \(\mathbf{Q}\) (through the \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\) term) - The geometric capacitance \(\mathbf{C}\)

    The Capacitance Matrix in Detail

    The N×N capacitance matrix \(\mathbf{C}\) relates electrode potentials to electrode charges:

    \[\mathbf{Q}_{electrode} = \mathbf{C} \cdot \mathbf{V}\]

    But for systems with electrolyte present, there’s also a zero-voltage contribution:

    \[\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{Q}_{0V} + \mathbf{C} \cdot \mathbf{V}\]

    Symbol Meaning
    \(\mathbf{Q}\) Total charge on each electrode group
    \(\mathbf{Q}_{0V}\) Charge configuration at zero applied potential (influenced by electrolyte structure)
    \(\mathbf{C}\) Capacitance matrix; depends only on geometry and dielectric
    \(\mathbf{V}\) Applied potentials on each electrode group

    Physical meaning of \(C_{ij}\): How much charge on electrode \(i\) changes when electrode \(j\)’s potential changes, with all other potentials held constant.

    For a symmetric two-electrode capacitor: - \(C_{11} = C_{22}\) (diagonal elements) - \(C_{12} = C_{21}\) (off-diagonal; negative — raising \(V_1\) induces opposite charge on electrode 2)

    What you can compute from C: - Total capacitance: \(C_{total} = \sum_{i,j} C_{ij}\) - Differential capacitance: \(dQ/dV\) at given V - Potential distribution in complex geometries - Response to potential sweep (cyclic voltammetry-like)

    Why We Need Gaussian Smearing

    Point charges at the electrode surface create a singularity — as an electrolyte ion approaches the surface, the interaction energy diverges (Coulomb catastrophe). This makes the matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) ill-conditioned and difficult to invert.

    The solution: Smear each electrode charge over a Gaussian distribution:

    \[\rho_i(\mathbf{r}) = q_i \left(\frac{\eta_i}{\pi}\right)^{3/2} \exp(-\eta_i |\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{R}_i|^2)\]

    The smearing width η (eta) controls:

    η (large) η (small)
    Narrow distribution (more like point charge) Wide distribution (smoother, better conditioned)
    More accurate electrostatics near surface Better matrix convergence; smoother potential
    Harder to converge (singular matrix) Easier to solve

    Typical η ≈ 1.0–2.0 Å⁻¹ (real units) or 0.2–0.5 (metal units).

    Physical interpretation: Smearing mimics the fact that real metals have a finite electron density at the surface — the charges are not point-like. The Gaussian width parameter should be chosen to match the electron gas density of the electrode material.

    Constant Potential vs. Constant Charge

    Aspect Constant Potential (electrode/conp) Constant Charge (electrode/conq)
    What is fixed Voltage on each electrode group Total charge on each electrode group
    What fluctuates Electrode atom charges Electrode potentials
    Physical analog Potentiostat Galvanostat
    Use case Electrochemical experiments Battery discharge (known charge flux)
    Output Induced charges Measured potentials
    Advantage Direct comparison to experiment Models known current
    Disadvantage Charge can spike during fast dynamics Potential response may be unexpected

    When to Use Each

    Constant potential (electrode/conp): - Comparing to electrochemical experiments (CV, EIS) - Studying double-layer structure at controlled voltage - Capacitance measurements - Modeling supercapacitors or electrochemical capacitors

    Constant charge (electrode/conq): - Modeling battery cycling (known current → known charge change) - When you know the amount of charge injected/extracted - Studying potential response to charging/discharging - Simulating galvanostatic experiments

    The Physical Chemistry of Electrode Interfaces

    Double Layer Structure

    The electric double layer has characteristic regions:

    1. Inner layer (Helmholtz): specifically adsorbed ions, water molecules. Distance ~0.3–0.5 nm from surface.
    2. Outer layer (Helmholtz): solvated ions attracted by coulomb but not specifically adsorbed. Distance ~0.5–1 nm.
    3. Diffuse layer: decaying ion concentration into bulk. Distance ~1–10 nm depending on ionic strength.

    CPM captures all of these — ion density profiles near electrodes reflect the balance of electrostatic attraction and ionic excluded-volume effects.

    Solvent Structure Near Electrodes

    Water molecules near electrodes: - Orient with H atoms toward negative electrode, O toward positive - Form layers (oscillating density) near the surface - This structure affects capacitance (Frumkin correction)

    The force field (water model) determines how realistic this structure is. Different water models (SPC/E, TIP4P) give different capacitances.

    How Capacitance Depends on Physical Parameters

    Parameter Effect on Capacitance Physical Reason
    Ion size Larger ions → lower capacitance Excluded volume reduces available space
    Ion charge Higher charge → stronger adsorption Different capacitance peak position
    Electrode structure Sharp features → field enhancement Higher local capacitance at tips/edges
    Water model dielectric Higher ε → higher capacitance Better screening reduces electrostatic penalty
    Temperature Higher T → lower capacitance Thermal disruption of ordered structure
    Potential Non-linear C(V) Double layer saturation at high V

    Comparison to Other Polarizable Methods

    Method What It Does When to Use How CPM Differs
    Drude oscillators Adds extra particle to model electron cloud polarizability Metals, polarizable anions CPM controls boundary potential, not internal polarizability
    Core-shell Adds massless shell for ionic polarizability Polarizable ions (Ca²⁺) CPM is for electrode boundaries
    QEq (charge equilibration) Redistributes charges within a single molecule based on electronegativity Organic molecules, chemical reactions CPM handles electrode-electrolyte boundary
    Fixed charge No polarizability Insulators, vacuum Wrong for metals

    CPM and Density Functional Theory

    CPM is a classical method. It does not compute electronic structure. For:

    Level Method What It Gives
    Classical MD CPM Electrode charges, double layer structure
    DFTB DFT-based tight binding Electronic structure at interface
    QM/MM Quantum region + classical MM Accurate reaction at electrode
    CPMD Car-Parrinello MD Full electronic structure (expensive)

    CPM assumptions: - Electrode charges respond instantly to electrostatic environment (adiabatic) - Metal is treated as a perfect conductor (except for smearing parameter) - No chemical bonding between electrode and electrolyte

    When CPM may fail: - Charge transfer across interface (faradaic currents) - Chemical reactions at electrode surface - Very sharp tips where quantum tunneling matters - Highlycorrelated electrolytes (ionic liquids at high charge)
